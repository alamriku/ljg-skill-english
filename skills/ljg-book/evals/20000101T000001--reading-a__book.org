#+SUBTITLE: Anthony Weston | What happens to your claim once you go looking for reasons

* I already support this - what is left to argue

You are about to tell people that more students should take part in international exchange. Young people leave their own country, meet a different way of living, and let the people there meet them. With more understanding on both sides, countries have a chance of carrying less suspicion and hostility.

Your reasons for supporting it feel natural. Someone who only ever meets others through news, rumour and received opinion easily reduces them to a label. Having lived alongside them, having heard them explain their own choices, some of those earlier judgments may loosen. Giving more young people that chance seems worth the money and the organizing effort.

So you start drafting an article. You think about how it should open, which words carry the meaning of exchange, how to make the last paragraph land. The conclusion you already have: support the expansion.

But suppose a reader says that they too want people from different countries to understand each other - they are simply not sure that expanding this programme would achieve it. What do you explain to them?

Saying again how important mutual understanding is does not seem to help. They agree with the goal. Their hesitation sits between the goal and the means: once a student is abroad, what changes? Do those changes outlast the programme? What level of investment do they justify?

You begin to notice that most of what you had prepared expresses why you like the vision. It has not yet shown why this particular proposal deserves to be accepted.

That is where this book starts to do work. Weston wrote a handbook of nine chapters and forty-five rules for supporting a conclusion with reasons. It begins with short arguments that a few sentences can carry, assembles short arguments into extended ones, and ends with writing an essay and speaking in public. Two appendices, on fallacies and on definitions, help find errors in reasoning and in wording.

That arrangement carries an important order: first work out what you actually have reason to believe, then hand the reasons that convinced you over to others to check. An initial position can be the starting point, but the inquiry has to be allowed to change it. Follow the exchange proposal far enough and the essay you finally write is likely to differ from the one you were about to write.

* Unfold one reason and there are reasons underneath it

Write down the suggestion you want to support: the international student exchange programme should be expanded. In this setting that sentence is the conclusion - the one that needs support. Then write what you think supports it: students who go abroad come to understand other cultures better; they also help their hosts understand their country better; mutual understanding helps people live and work together.

The sentences brought in to support the conclusion are the premises. Both names describe the job a sentence does in this argument. Neither exempts a premise from being checked.

Suppose the reader stops at the first one: how do you know that going abroad makes a student understand another culture better? A new argumentative task has appeared. The sentence that was supporting your proposal now needs support itself.

Weston asks you to unfold that layer too. You can look for the experience of students who took part, read surveys and studies, and ask people who have actually organized exchanges. You have to show how those experiences and materials support "exchange increases understanding". If the support is still thin, the sentence has to be rewritten - perhaps the original proposal does too.

This is the structure that lets a short paragraph grow into an essay: a sentence that is a reason at one level becomes, at the next level down, a conclusion needing proof. A complex argument is built from these interdependent small ones. Each has to pass two checks: are the reasons offered reliable, and are they enough to support what is being claimed?

The basic argument for student exchange is not finished. Even if exchange does increase understanding, and understanding does help cooperation, it still has to be shown that the benefit justifies the programme's cost and risk. Folding in the worries the book raises later, you can sketch an argument still to be completed:

#+begin_example
Proposal: expand international student exchange
|
+-- it achieves the expected benefit
|   +-- exchange helps both sides understand
|   |   +-- participants' experience, surveys, studies
|   +-- mutual understanding helps coexistence
|       +-- explain the process, find real grounds
|
+-- the benefit justifies the cost and the risk
|   +-- funding, student safety, host arrangements
|
+-- it is still worth choosing against the alternatives
    +-- can the partners and the format be adjusted
#+end_example

Take the branch "exchange helps both sides understand". The experience and surveys underneath should support that sentence; that sentence, with the others, supports expanding the programme. If all you can find is material showing students enjoyed being abroad, the branch is still not fastened: between "enjoyed it" and "understands the people there better" lies a relation still to be shown. However sincerely the proposal above is written, that gap does not close itself.

The main branches cannot stand in for one another either. Good material about cultural understanding cannot answer whether the programme is affordable, and thorough safety measures cannot by themselves show the exchange achieved its purpose. Now you know where each line of questioning belongs, and you can add to or revise each separately.

There is no need to question endlessly. Weston allows you to start from premises that are reliable, relevant and acceptable to the parties, and to spend the effort where the real disagreement is and where it affects the conclusion. An argument needs to be adequate to the question at hand. It does not require proving everything in the world before you may say one sentence.

That gives the book's methods their place. Generalization helps you move from limited experience to a wider range; the rules on sources help you judge whether borrowed knowledge can be relied on; causal argument checks whether an arrangement really produced the change; analogy checks whether existing experience carries over; deduction checks what given premises actually yield. Each handles a different support problem on that diagram, and together they let a claim gradually stand.

* You go looking for evidence and find you had not asked precisely

Stay on the sentence you feel most confident about: exchange increases understanding.

Success stories come to mind first. Participants come back and talk about the people they met, about what they had misunderstood before, and that experience does matter. Weston explicitly suggests finding such examples for the exchange proposal. But one experience tells us the change can happen, while expanding the programme means placing that expectation on many more people in many more places. You need to know how far those examples reach.

Follow the requirement: if you only reached the people most willing to share, what happened to the quieter ones? If the material comes only from long-established programmes, would one just being set up achieve the same? If participants came back satisfied, is the satisfaction about an enjoyable trip, or about a more accurate picture of a life that had been unfamiliar?

These are questions to investigate. They do not announce in advance that exchange fails. They turn your vague plan to "find some good examples" into a far clearer requirement: look at what happened and at what did not; look at those who succeeded and at how those people entered the material in the first place.

The rules on generalization now work together. A single example usually cannot carry a judgment about many people; many examples still have to be checked for representativeness. A count of successes means little without the total; a claim of improvement means little without the state before. A figure means what the question it answers makes it mean. Without something to compare against, a precise number may still not establish what you wanted to establish.

Then the question of sources becomes concrete. Organizers may know the details of the arrangements, participating students can speak to their own experience, researchers may hold a wider comparison. Who has relevant experience on this question, and who produced material that can be checked, is more useful than a general appeal to experts.

At the same time, organizers want the programme to keep its support, and participants may not want to tell everyone about an unpleasant experience. Those possibilities are a reminder to cross-check. An interest in the outcome does not void everything someone observed; being closest to the programme does not mean their assessment of its effects is accepted whole. Provenance, qualifications, interests and other reliable sources are weighed together.

If the material comes from the web, the work is the same. A professional appearance is only an appearance; you have to follow the trail to who did the study, how the result was obtained, and whether the original claim was altered in the retelling. Once an argument starts drawing on facts, searching is only the entrance; reading and assessing the material is the work that follows.

Even with a credible comparison showing that students who took part understand other cultures better, one question has not gone away: was the difference produced by the exchange?

Here the book's requirements for causal argument apply to the same programme. Perhaps students already more interested in other cultures are likelier to apply; perhaps their family and schooling shaped both the application and the capacity to understand; perhaps the exchange does work and those prior conditions work too. Several explanations can fit the same surface result.

You are now facing two different jobs. The generalization asked whom the observation extends to; the causal check asks why the phenomenon occurred. Answering the first is not answering the second. If the goal is to produce more understanding by expanding the programme, the second cannot be skipped, because what you propose to change is the opportunity to take part.

Weston suggests filling in the process behind each plausible explanation and then finding material to compare them. A story that reads smoothly shows only that it deserves serious consideration. To judge that it actually happened, it has to face the alternatives. Once you find the effect depends on certain conditions, your earlier sentence - go abroad and you will understand - can be made more accurate: which kind of contact, under which arrangements, changed what in whom?

From here, the evidence is already changing your proposal. At first you only wanted more places. Now you also have to care whether the conditions that actually do the work survive the increase.

* The same proposal gets more specific the longer it is discussed

You still want to expand exchange, but you are no longer willing to count every form of going abroad as the same opportunity. That change reaches your wording.

If the opening says "understanding other cultures", the middle slides into "enjoying travel", and the ending becomes "promoting world peace", all three sound admirable while the reader has been carried across several unproven steps. That is why Weston asks for consistent terms in the key places. The same word helps everyone recognize the same thing, and a necessary distinction makes the missing argument visible.

Once the terms are clear, there is still the temptation to win by naming. Calling a programme "peace exchange" does not show it promotes peace; calling an opponent "closed-minded" does not answer their doubt about the programme's effects. The appendix on definitions guards exactly that line: a definition lets both sides know what is being discussed, and conclusions about good, effect and action still need reasons.

Where the definition itself is contested, Weston suggests starting from clear cases on each side, then handling the borderline and explaining why the line falls there; for a particular purpose, a workable criterion can be given. Brought to the proposal in front of you, that means saying which kind of programme is under discussion, rather than mixing every cross-border activity together - and certainly not writing the hoped-for benefits into the definition.

Even with the terms clear, a gap in the reasoning can remain. You might say: if exchange really deepens understanding, participants should understand other cultures better afterwards; they do understand other cultures better; so the exchange worked. No word has shifted, yet the conclusion crossed a step: as noted, the difference may have existed before they applied.

Using a conditional backwards shuts out the other possible causes. The rules of deduction let that error be named: from "if A then B" together with B, A does not follow on its own. To use the conditional soundly toward B, A has to be established first - and whether A holds is answered by the evidence.

When deciding whether to support the programme, you can also write the condition out: if a programme of this kind does achieve its goal, the benefit justifies the cost, and it is the better choice among workable options, then support it. Only after showing this programme meets those conditions may support follow.

The "if ... then ..." itself has to be defended, or accepted by both sides as the standard for the discussion. The form will not establish that the condition is reasonable, still less that the programme in front of you meets it. It only guarantees that with true premises and a valid step, the conclusion cannot conflict with the premises.

The book calls that basic step modus ponens. The other deductive forms go on to handle how conditions chain, how a consequence can be used to rule a condition out, how options are eliminated, and how a contradiction reveals a problem in an assumption. A long argument can be built from these smaller steps, but every join has to keep the original conditions, without quietly reversing a direction or dropping a restriction.

Eliminating options, for instance, only has force when the options are stated fully enough. If you write the choice as "expand this programme, or go on misunderstanding each other", then even if the second is unacceptable, the first does not follow automatically. Other partners and other formats very likely exist. The appendix lists collapsing the road into two as a false dilemma; the name tells you where to look, and the actual repair is still to find the possibility that was left out.

Seen this way, expression, definition and logic are all helping with the same difficult job: claiming only the part you have actually given reasons for. They also make room for the next step. The clearer the proposal, the more precisely others can say where they disagree - and the better your chance of answering the real objection.

* Objections arrive and the original proposal starts to change

On student exchange, Weston develops one objection seriously: young people are inexperienced, may lack protection in unfamiliar surroundings, and the programme puts them at risk.

If you only write "some people worry about safety", it is easy to go on to say your own concern matters more, or to restate how valuable understanding is. But if you stop and state the objection in full, the situation is different. What the other side fears is a particular young person, in an unfamiliar place, meeting exploitation, harm or conflict without timely help.

Stated that way, the objection is not an obstacle to be cleared. It names a condition your proposal has to meet. Expanding the programme now has to include who is responsible for the host arrangements, how a participant reaches help, and what happens when something goes wrong.

So the proposal changes shape. It is no longer "send more students abroad" but "expand a programme that keeps the conditions under which the exchange works and the protections a participant can actually reach". The claim has become narrower and more defensible at once.

Weston's point about objections is that they are part of the inquiry rather than an interruption of it. An objection you cannot answer is telling you where the argument is thin. An objection you can answer, answered in the open, gives the reader the one thing a confident restatement cannot: a reason to think you looked.

* Sometimes serious research gets you only as far as "possible"

The book sets the discussion of extraterrestrial life beside the exchange proposal, and the contrast is useful because the two end in different places.

The reasoning runs through steps that can each be examined: there are many stars; many have planets; some planets are in a range where life as we know it could persist. Each step can be given real grounds, and together they support the conclusion that life elsewhere is possible.

What they do not yet support is that it has been found. Between "the conditions could exist in many places" and "it is there" lies a step nothing in the chain has taken. You can argue all the way to a well-grounded "possible" and still have to say that the next question is open.

That is a different ending from the exchange case, and it is worth sitting with. An argument that reaches "possible, and here is why" has done its work honestly. Writing it up as "almost certainly" to make the ending stronger would quietly spend credit the evidence never earned.

* By the time it becomes an essay, you have gained something

Now the article can be written, and it will not be the one you started.

Weston's advice on the essay follows from everything before it: the main point is stated, the objections are taken up rather than avoided, and the structure of the argument is visible to the reader instead of hidden by the prose. What makes that possible is that you have the structure - the branches, the gaps you closed, the ones you narrowed the claim to avoid.

Speaking in public adds its own constraints. A listener cannot reread, so the main point and the order have to be carried in the voice; the examples have to be few enough to stay in mind; the objection has to be stated fairly, because a room can tell when it has not been.

Both chapters rest on the same thing. What you can say clearly to a reader or a room is what you worked out when nobody was watching.

* The support you wanted now needs others to judge it

Go back to the beginning. You wanted to persuade someone of a proposal you had already accepted. You now have a narrower proposal, a set of reasons that can be checked, a safety condition written into the plan, and some honest gaps.

That reader who agreed with the goal but doubted the means can finally be answered - not by restating the vision, but by showing which kind of programme, under which arrangements, has which effect, at what cost, and what remains uncertain.

And the judgment is no longer yours alone to make. Having given reasons that can be examined, you have also handed others the means to examine them. That is the exchange the book is really about.

* What stays with you afterwards

Weston wants you to find out what you have reason to believe before you set out to convince anyone, which means letting the inquiry change the claim you began with; once you can say which reason supports which step and where the support runs out, the proposal you defend is narrower, more useful and genuinely open to someone else's judgment.
