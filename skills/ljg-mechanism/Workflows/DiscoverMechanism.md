# From a phenomenon to a grounded mechanism

The research loops around one object: observation raises a hypothesis, the hypothesis makes a distinguishing prediction, material tests the prediction, and the result returns to the observation or the process. The four stages correct each other in both directions; when new evidence changes the object or the scope, rebuild the explanation instead of forcing the old one back on.

## Step 0 - Scope and sufficiency of material

Read the phenomenon, the material and the current context the user supplied, and separate first-hand observation, hearsay, generalization and claims still to be tested. Pin down which outcome the user wants explained, and avoid swapping an income question for a productivity question, or research into a phenomenon for an action plan.

When an object, metric, time window or comparison baseline that would change which mechanism applies is missing, first search for public material that can fill it in; where an assumption is safe and workable, state it in one sentence and continue. Ask for clarification only when different readings would make it a materially different research object and the available material cannot resolve it — then ask for the minimum necessary. Unknown personal experience, internal organizational processes and private data must never be filled in as facts from industry averages or teaching hypotheticals.

Online by default. Use the search and content-fetching tools this environment actually provides; never claim to have called a tool that does not exist or that a search has already been done. Search queries carry only the general information the research needs — never send the user's private material to an external service.

## 1 - Spot the anomaly: separate observation from expectation

This stage should yield an accurate observation, an expectation that can be interrogated, and the difference between the two that genuinely needs explaining.

- Observation: which object, over what range and period, relative to which baseline, showed what change? What data supports it?
- Expectation: which conditions were assumed to produce which results? Which omitted relation does that expectation rely on?
- Difference: the expected condition is missing yet the result appears; the condition is present yet the result does not; or the same conditions produce different results.

Do not manufacture surprise. How a familiar kind of order sustains itself, or what hidden function a familiar tool performs, can be a perfectly good puzzle.

Check the scope and scale of the phenomenon first. Search for observable metrics, raw data and counter-cases, and look for selective samples, a changed denominator, a substituted metric, and the time scale. Inflating "some people do" into "everyone does", or "no effect yet in the short run" into "never works", creates the wrong explanatory task.

When the original generalization does not hold, say which facts survive and how the research question changes, then study the difference that actually exists. Never explain a phenomenon that is not there just to complete the framework.

## 2 - Trace the process: land the explanation on actions and intermediate changes

Pick a concrete object that can carry the question and trace "who perceived what / why they adjusted / which quantity changes first / how it propagates to the final result". It may include feedback, constraints, selection, lags or allocation; do not force every mechanism into a linear causal chain.

Key concepts need a recognizable referent, and intermediate links need an observable address: orders, closing prices, waiting times, rework rates, decision records, where resources flow, what participants do. Where an intermediate record cannot be obtained, mark it clearly as inference.

Teaching hypotheticals help the reader run the relations; their data, people, events and outcomes must be labelled as hypothetical on the spot. A hypothetical that computes correctly is not evidence that the mechanism holds in reality.

Put forward the mechanism hypothesis with the most explanatory power, and keep the competing explanations that could claim the same outcome. Candidates must differ in their relations or their conditions, not merely rename the same mechanism. A small number of candidates is usually enough; the count follows the real disagreements in the question.

For each candidate, say which key conditions it depends on, which change it predicts will appear first, and which observation would weaken or overturn it. Several mechanisms can coexist; the task is then to say which link, which kind of object, and which time range each one acts on.

## 3 - Separate the explanations: let online material discriminate

### Distinguishing predictions

Before filtering candidates with material, write down the predictions that are easy to rationalize after the fact: if the hypothesis holds, what will be seen first; what different result each competing explanation gives; which observation would make you change your mind. New mechanisms can be proposed as evidence arrives, but a prediction written after seeing the result is not prior verification.

If every candidate predicts the same result under current conditions, they cannot be distinguished yet. Look for a new place to observe or a change of conditions; never decide on the grounds that one explanation reads better or cites more.

### Make the search hit the load-bearing relations

Search serves both the verification of the phenomenon and the key causal links and competing explanations. Around the most discriminating relation, alternate searches for support, counterexamples, opposite results and alternative mechanisms; do not keep searching only the vocabulary of your preferred hypothesis.

| What is being tested | More useful material |
|---|---|
| Whether the phenomenon occurred and whom it affects | Raw statistics, metric definitions, full study results, checkable records |
| Whether the intermediate link changes as predicted | Behavioural or transaction records, process data, field observation, primary experiments |
| Whether one factor causes the result | Randomized experiments, credible quasi-experiments or explicit causal identification; observational studies need their confounding and selection limits stated |
| Which competing explanation is closer to the facts | Comparisons on the same basis, changes in a key condition, differences in timing, cases the original model does not explain |
| Whether it transfers to another setting | Independent samples with stated conditions, tests in a different context, reverse cases |

Primary material first: the original papers, data and methods, official metrics and standards, direct process records. Commentary and reviews help find sources and suggest candidates; a direct participant's account has value, but weigh their vantage point and their interests.

Once an important source is found, read the relevant body text, methods or data notes and check the objects, period, comparison groups, metrics and actual conclusions. If you only have the abstract or a search snippet, limit the support to the readable part and say which method or mechanism details you could not check; never treat content behind a paywall as read.

Keep the publication date separate from the dates of the events studied. A newer paper does not necessarily study the most recent period; where public facts may have changed, check against current material. Different pages retelling the same study are still one piece of evidence, and agreement between models is not independent evidence about reality.

### Land the evidence on the relations

For every load-bearing relation, check what the source actually measured, how the authors inferred from it, and what your explanation adds on top. Material that establishes a change in outcome has not necessarily measured the mediating process, and an aggregate correlation does not establish individual causation.

Material is worth what it can change in the judgment. You can keep the short table below internally and surface only the most important rows in the deliverable:

| Relation or candidate | Distinguishing prediction | Actual observation and direct source | Scope of support / alternative explanations / remaining gaps |
|---|---|---|---|

Never write "not significant", "nothing found", or a null result from an underpowered test as proof that the mechanism does not exist. Check whether an opposite result falls inside the previously stated scope; when the conditions match and the prediction fails, revise the relation rather than the wording.

### Updating and stopping

Evidence may keep, downgrade, rule out or rebuild a candidate. Conflicts can come from mechanisms coexisting, different objects, different periods, or a wrong scope at the start — never average a conflict into a vague conclusion.

When a counterexample forces the scope to narrow, say where the original prediction failed, what the new boundary rests on, and what distinguishing result the new explanation implies. Continually adding exceptions without changing any prediction is protecting the explanation, not making the mechanism more solid.

Stop at a judgment with enough grounds for the current scope, or at a key gap you have identified and public search cannot fill. When the key relations have support and the main competing explanations have been compared, do not search again just to add links. When new searches keep returning retellings of the same source and the key distinction depends on data that cannot be obtained, deliver the limits and the single most valuable next observation.

Express the strength of evidence through the load-bearing relations and the power to discriminate, not through a subjective score or a link count:

- **Supported within the stated scope**: the key process has real grounds, the causal identification or process evidence carries the wording used, and the important competing explanations have been tested; state the uncertainty that remains.
- **Current leading explanation**: it explains the existing observations, but key causal or discriminating evidence is still missing; say why it leads and what exactly is missing.
- **Not yet distinguishable**: several mechanisms still explain the same observations; keep them side by side and name the next piece of data that would best separate them.
- **The original observation needs rewriting**: verification showed a wrong scope or an overreaching generalization; study the corrected object and keep whatever in the user's observation still holds.

A researcher's assessment of an overall conclusion does not automatically raise the strength of each individual relation. When the network is unavailable, you can deliver only reasoning and a test design; do not say "verified" or claim a solid empirically grounded mechanism.

## 4 - Transfer with conditions: check the explanation implies new results

From the concrete object, abstract "under these conditions, through this relation, this result is produced". Keep the actors, the information and resource constraints, the direction of effect and the time scale; stripping away the domain name must not strip away the load-bearing conditions.

First change one key condition within the same object and write down which quantity you expect to move first and what the final result becomes. This counterfactual tests the internal relations; until it is carried out or independent data arrives, it remains reasoning.

Then use a different situation to check whether the objects and variables correspond, whether the key conditions are present, and what prediction follows. An analogy, or a hypothetical whose answer has already been revealed, only shows an understandable transfer path; without independent real-world material it cannot be upgraded into a cross-domain law.

Whether a mechanism is worth taking away depends on whether it changes a new prediction, judgment or action. When the user asked only for an explanation, give them the handles for judging; do not turn it into an execution plan, an experiment, a publication or a persisted artifact on your own.

## Delivery check

The deliverable should answer the phenomenon directly, explain the relations in a runnable way, and give the key sources, the comparison with competing explanations, the conditions of application and the door for revision. Mark facts, inferences, conjectures and unknowns where they change the judgment — do not carpet every sentence with evidence labels.

A short answer need not print all four steps; a full demonstration unfolds all four. End with the most reliable mechanism judgment available and the one observation that would overturn it. What you completed is research within the stated scope; limited material cannot establish a general law.
