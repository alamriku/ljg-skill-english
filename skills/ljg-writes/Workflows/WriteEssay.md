# WriteEssay

Turn an idea into an article the reader can understand, use and question. The steps below are for analysis and checking; the order, tone and rhythm of the body follow the content and the reader.

## Step 0 - Decide the scale of delivery

Default length is 700-1100 words. When the user asks for a short edit, a line, a title, or a specific length, deliver that. A short edit keeps the core meaning and the necessary qualifications; do not add cases or claims to satisfy the long-form checks that follow.

Confirm the input contains a claim that can be argued with. If there is only a topic and no judgment, extract a candidate judgment from the context; ask only when the candidates would lead to completely different articles. When rewriting existing text, protect the meaning, facts and tone first, and do not strengthen the conclusion on your own.

## Step 1 - State the judgment to be discussed

Write the idea in one plain sentence. Internally you can use this frame to check the relation is clear:

```text
Under [condition], [A] changes [B] through [mechanism], producing [result].
```

That sentence is a working check on the idea. While analyzing the case you may correct its condition, mechanism or result. If the relation is still unclear, keep working it out.

## Step 2 - Choose a case that is easy to follow

Pick a case whose background is easy to follow and whose key causation is complete, so the reader sees what happened, how, and with what result. Before writing, check the correspondence:

```text
abstract role A = who or what in the case
abstract role B = who or what in the case
key effect      = the action that actually happens in the case
visible result  = the change the reader can observe
```

When they do not correspond, change the case. When simplifying: if deleting a feedback loop, a sequence or a constraint would change the conclusion, that item stays.

Prefer explaining around one case, so the reader does not have to learn a new background repeatedly. Add a related case only when one cannot carry the key relation. A case shows how a relation happens; a general factual claim still needs evidence. A hypothetical case must be visibly hypothetical — never written as something you witnessed or as an established fact.

## Step 3 - Check what the existing explanation misses

Consider the explanation the reader may already hold: under it, how would they judge, what would they do, what would they expect? Check whether it has any of these problems:

- it cannot explain one step of the case;
- it makes a wrong prediction;
- it treats as one case two situations that should be distinguished;
- it succeeds but ignores a cost.

These problems must be findable in the case, the factual material, or the logic — never fabricated to introduce a concept. When the existing explanation is already adequate, go straight to Step 6 and state the full relation.

## Step 4 - Introduce the concept the moment requires

Find the concept, distinction or relation that explains the current problem. Give its meaning in everyday words first, then return to the case and see how it changes the explanation, the prediction or the action. Introduce the term when precise reference is needed. A concept changes our understanding of the case; the actual outcome changes only if the actions or conditions change with it.

Check four things:

1. Why is this concept needed?
2. What exactly does it distinguish or change?
3. Reanalyzing the case with it, what changes in the explanation, prediction or action?
4. What is still not explained?

When several unfamiliar concepts crowd into one sentence, unfold them in the order understanding requires. Explain the one needed next, make it clear through the case, then decide whether to add more.

## Step 5 - Keep pressing on what is still unexplained

Continue analyzing, where needed, until the key relation is clear. Useful questions:

- How does A lead to B — is something missing in between?
- Which premises does the judgment depend on, and when they change, what fails first?
- Is the result caused by A, or is another variable doing the work?

Further analysis should make the explanation more accurate and concrete, or the conditions clearer. If you are only swapping in a more abstract word, go back to the facts and the concrete process. The conclusion may be intuitive, and it may stop at a question the available material cannot answer.

When several factors interact, happen at once, or jointly shift the probability of an outcome, show the processes side by side, list the state changes, or use two related cases to show how they jointly affect the result.

## Step 6 - State the full relation

Once the concepts are clear, return to the original case and check that the whole process holds together:

```text
initial condition -> how the factors act or constrain each other -> observable change -> final result
```

Say what problem each concept solved, how they relate, and how the analysis supports or corrects the original judgment. Avoid setting up suspense paragraph after paragraph while never making the sequence of events clear.

Go back to the working sentence from Step 1 and correct or extend its mechanism, conditions and boundaries in light of the analysis. If the original sentence is already accurate, keep it — do not rewrite it just to show the analysis did something.

## Step 7 - Check other situations and counterexamples

Run two internal tests before writing:

1. *Transfer*: swap the people, the industry and the surface material, and use the same relation to explain a markedly different phenomenon.
2. *Counterexample*: find a situation that looks the same but does not actually fit the relation, and say which condition makes the difference.

If all you can do is retell the original case, check whether surface similarity has been mistaken for causation. When no counterexample can be found, keep checking the conditions of application rather than covering the gap with more examples of the same kind.

Transfer and counterexample need not both appear in the body, but the body must leave at least one ground for transfer or one boundary of failure. A case cannot substitute for factual evidence; where external facts are involved, give the evidence or scope the claim precisely.

## Step 8 - Write it as a coherent article

Let the content decide the reading order. You may state the judgment first and then explain it, or start from a concrete fact or case and let the judgment emerge from the explanation. Develop the shortcomings of an existing explanation only when they genuinely matter; not every piece needs the story of "intuition fails, concept arrives, discovery follows".

Each paragraph develops one idea. The first sentence can be a judgment, a fact, an action, or a pick-up from the previous paragraph; the rest completes the idea. To state a judgment, say the claim plainly, then the reasons and the conditions; where it sits follows the content. Factual negation and necessary distinctions can stay — just avoid denying one formulation and revealing another to manufacture a turn.

Paragraphs must connect in substance: what did the previous one settle, and why does the next one follow? Where that connection is missing, reorder or supply the reasoning instead of papering over the jump with "more importantly".

End when the question has been answered. You may point to a practical consequence, a use, or a limit; there is no need to manufacture an aphorism, a reversal or a grand realization.

Once written, confirm that the specific reader can answer from the body alone:

1. What claim does the article make, and why does it hold? If an old explanation was discussed, where did it fall short?
2. What do the key concepts refer to in reality, and what does each explain?
3. Which condition produces which result through which mechanism?
4. Where else can this judgment be used, and where does it fail?

If answering requires extra explanation from the author, go back and supply the missing clues in the body — do not paste in the analysis checklist.

## Techniques available

Use as needed; none of them is required:

- *Before and after*: return to the same case and compare the new explanation with the old one.
- *Question as heading*: when the next section really answers a specific question, use the reader's own words as the heading.
- *Process or state table*: when prose struggles to relate several factors, show the process and the before/after states directly.
- *Analogy*: use something familiar to aid understanding; state the correspondence, and never let the analogy replace evidence.
- *Conditions stated nearby*: put an important limit next to the claim it qualifies, so the reader can judge.

## Step 9 - Check the content, then revise the prose

Check the content first:

- Is the existing explanation presented accurately, or simplified into something easy to knock down?
- Is every new concept necessary, and does it explain a specific problem?
- What changed in the explanation, prediction or action once the concept arrived? Has a change in understanding been written up as a change in the real outcome?
- Was a problem manufactured to add a layer, or several unfamiliar concepts crammed into one sentence?
- Is the key relation complete, and are the case, the evidence, the scope of application and the boundary clear?

Then read the whole piece and revise the prose, following "Stance and language" in `SKILL.md`. Focus on:

- *Meaning and collocation*: are the words accurate, do subject and action fit, are the references clear? When a synonym still reads badly, rewrite the sentence from its meaning.
- *Tone and proportion*: does it suit the purpose? Is anything forced into colloquialism or false profundity, or a conjecture turned into an assertion? Keep the necessary qualifications and never invent confidence figures.
- *Sentences and paragraphs*: are cause and condition stated fully? Are there choppy fragments, deeply nested sentences, or repeated parallelism and contrast? Adjust by meaning, not by a word or frequency quota.
- *Joins across the piece*: does it read straight through? Do the paragraphs connect, and are the terms, metaphors and tone consistent? When a local edit breaks the context, rewrite the paragraph.

Leave what is already clear and natural. Save only the revised complete draft; do not pick the best lines out of two drafts and stitch them together. Finally, confirm the article really explains the judgment and tells the reader how to use it and when to be careful; a counterintuitive conclusion is not required.
