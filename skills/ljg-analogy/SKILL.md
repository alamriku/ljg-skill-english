# ljg-analogy: make the relation visible in another setting

The user gives a scenario; you deliver an analogy that first shows a picture, then lets the relation be recognized, and finally leaves the reader understanding the original scenario better. The objects may be far apart as long as the relations line up closely. Novelty does not offset distortion, and a familiar object can carry a surprising relation.

Terminology runs one way: **the scenario the user wants to understand is the target domain; the scenario borrowed to help understand it is the base domain.** Search goes from target to base; knowledge transfer comes back from base to target. If the user's wording is reversed, work from their actual intent instead of interrupting the work over terminology.

## Workflow routing

| Request | What to do |
|------|------|
| A scenario, with a request for an analogy or metaphor | Deliver one main analogy to the standard below |
| Comparing two objects, products or mechanisms | Use one shared relational frame that keeps both the common ground and the key differences |
| A specified audience, domain, style, or "just one line" | Keep the structural constraints and follow the user's presentation request |
| Several options, or a comparison of existing candidates | Compare different relational focuses, name the recommendation and the trade-offs |

## Gotchas

- "Both are complex", "both are networks", "both need balance" apply too widely to carry an analogy on their own. A relation says who does what to whom, which conditions constrain it, and how it produces a result or a layout.
- Pairing up several objects is just a dictionary. Prefer relations governed by one shared causal, constraint, proportional or spatial structure; a count of similarities is not systematicity.
- "Higher-order" means relations between relations. It does not mean more abstract or grander, and it is not restricted to causal chains. Proportional, spatial and emotional relations can bear weight too.
- Roles stay fixed within one mapping: do not let an object quietly change identity because it is convenient for the next sentence. If a different facet genuinely needs comparing, say you are switching to another mapping. Surface attributes do not transfer along with the relation.
- The base domain is something you found; the target-domain facts come from the user. Do not invent motives, causes or outcomes in the target domain to make the figure work; a gap can only appear as an explicit assumption.
- An analogy yields candidate inferences — it does not prove the target domain must be that way. In particular, never derive the necessity, legitimacy, or someone's intent in a social matter from a natural process.

## Is the input enough

You need at least to recognize what is happening in the scenario and which relation the user wants lit up. If there is one word and no recognizable situation, ask one question that would change the choice of analogy; if the word has several common referents, confirm which one is meant, and do not let the options in your question presuppose a reading. When a usable scenario is already there, get to work. With no audience specified, write for an ordinary reader and prefer everyday settings that need little explanation to picture.

Aim the analogy at the understanding task the reader is doing right now: what to recognize, what to tell apart, or which change to predict. Purpose, composition, how something works, and what it feels like are different explanatory focuses; when a reader asks how two things differ, summarizing each one's purpose may miss the question. Choose the focus from the question and the existing context, and where necessary say "the thing to watch here is ..." in one line, rather than turning this check into another round of questions to the user.

Goals, differences in power, sequence and constraints the user stressed must not vanish in abstraction. Versions and states are part of an object's identity too: when new evidence changes a role, a process or a dependency, re-examine the framing and the main analogy. If the original candidate can no longer preserve the load-bearing relation, replace it rather than tacking a note about version differences onto the end.

## What makes an analogy worth delivering

**It fits the task.** The mapping preserves the relation the reader needs this time. A figure may explain some feature of the object and still be irrelevant to the question at hand; what matters is whether it improves the current recognition, distinction or understanding.

**The relation holds.** The same informative relational skeleton exists on both sides. The reader can put each person or object back into its role and check the direction of effect, the order, the dependencies and the feedback. Explaining only part is allowed, but a vague "it is only an analogy" must not cover a load-bearing relation that runs the opposite way.

**The relations hang together.** There is a set of mutually supporting correspondences, not a pile of similarities collected to fill a quota. If a change in one place moves another, say how that dependency recurs on both sides. When a simple proportional or spatial correspondence is enough, do not bolt on causation or pad the relation count.

**The picture stands on its own.** The base domain has concrete objects and actions an ordinary reader can follow without first being taught a new theory. A constructed scene explicitly marked as hypothetical is fine; inventing a natural mechanism, a historical event or a story about a real person and presenting it as fact is not.

**It shows something extra.** The analogy makes at least one previously unremarkable relation visible: a blind spot, a constraint, a cost, a consequence, or the condition that separates two judgments. That gain must follow from the mapping. To check an explanatory analogy, use a small situation that does not simply copy the original description and see whether it helps the reader tell the difference, predict a result, or choose the next step; a candidate that can only restate names and features is still weak. This is an internal content check — it need not become a quiz for the user, and it is not the same as testing a real reader. A literary metaphor may change how a reader sees a relation without being forced through an operational test or a scientific experiment.

**It survives comparison.** When several candidates work, compare them against the same understanding task: which preserves the more critical relation, which supports a more specific distinction or inference, which needs less extra explanation. Only the vehicle's noun differing while the relational skeleton stays the same is not a second structure. Credible relations and an audience who can follow them are the floor; a familiar near-domain correspondence can beat a novel far-domain one, and distance by itself is not quality. Keep the exploration of candidates internal and deliver only the strongest option unless the user asked for more; if no candidate qualifies, say exactly what is missing.

**The boundary is specific.** Point out which striking feature of the base domain must not be carried over, or which change in a target-domain condition would break the correspondence. Do it in one sentence tied to this analogy, not as a generic disclaimer.

## When comparing two objects

Prefer a set of scenes from one familiar domain, describing both sides with the same roles and relations, so the common ground stays comparable and the key difference stands out. Finding an apt image for A and another for B separately still does not show how A and B differ; what can be borrowed is a set of operating relations that differ. If two base domains are genuinely more accurate, keep the shared basis of comparison rather than forcing one domain.

Check that the two correspondences actually discriminate: if swapping the vehicles and keeping the load-bearing relations unchanged still reads fine, they probably only hit a shared attribute. Put a new input or a changed condition into both sides; the reader should be able to see from the analogy how each handles it and what differs. If the different answers depend entirely on extra target-domain facts written in afterwards, the vehicle is not doing the work. Renaming nouns from the source, or stuffing good/bad labels into the figure, is no substitute for this check.

## How the deliverable reads

Deliver directly in the conversation by default; do not create notes, images or files automatically. Usually a few paragraphs take one main analogy all the way, with the length following the scenario:

- **Give the analogy first.** One vivid sentence that immediately says what is being compared to what. Carry the genuinely load-bearing relation into that sentence and avoid an empty "X is like Y".
- **Run the correspondence.** Make the concrete situation in the base domain clear, then connect it back to the user's scenario. A complex mapping can use a short table, with the rows saying how the relation corresponds — never noun paired with noun only.
- **Land on the added understanding.** Say which part of the original scenario is now clearer, and which part is most easily carried over by mistake. Do not simply restate the opening in different words.

These are information requirements, not a fixed heading template. When the user wants one line, deliver one line; when they want a clean piece they can quote, give the clean piece and keep the review language out of it. A metaphor may drop the "like", but the structural correspondence must still hold.

## Theory and the limits of fact

This skill borrows the relational mapping and systematicity principles from Gentner (1983). The understanding task, the two-object contrast, candidate elimination, audience familiarity, the gain in understanding and the specific boundary are design standards added here for a creative task; they are not claimed to come from the paper or to be validated by it.

For questions about definitions, non-causal relations, metaphor, or extrapolating beyond the theory, read [Theory.md](Theory.md). For maintenance or trial runs, use [EvalCases.json](EvalCases.json); those cases define inputs and acceptance grounds, not one correct figure.

Everyday constructed scenes need no internet. If the final analogy depends on an uncertain scientific, historical or industry fact, check it against primary material with whatever search tool is available; if you cannot check it, switch to a base domain whose facts are solid, or mark it explicitly as a hypothetical scene.

## Examples

- "I save a lot of articles, but when I actually have a problem I cannot find any of them. Give me a sharp analogy." The deliverable should light up the relation between putting things in and getting them out, mapped to a concrete situation — not praise the quantity with "a treasury of knowledge".
- "Both tools do the job: with one I open a session per item myself, the other takes the request from a main session, delegates and reports back. Explain the difference with an analogy." The deliverable must preserve the differences in entry point, routing and coordination; it must not flatten the two into "an execution tool and a storage container", and it must not assume one correct vehicle.
- "After years away from home, one phrase in the old accent makes me feel someone is waiting for me. Just one metaphor." The deliverable keeps the structure of sound, belonging and a relation being awakened, gives one line, and adds no table or action list.
