* {what the authors' method, relation or finding actually changed}

{Explain the key steps or acting relations, showing how input, processing and result connect. Explain a term where it first appears, and show how it works through the current example or another one. When you change examples, say which layer of the explanation the new one adds.}

{Before a key result, state the experiment's question, conditions and comparison; afterwards, explain what the result means and what limits it. Explain the most representative result first; other results may stay when they add different information, limit the conclusion or explain an exception, with the reason recorded in the research record - there is no fixed count of numbers.}

{Add sections as the explanation requires. They may progress or sit side by side; say why each is necessary and how it connects to the overall question. Never invent a causal link just to make the sections unswappable.}

{Put a figure where the reader needs it. ASCII diagrams go in an Org example block no wider than 80 display columns; after each diagram, walk a concrete example through the process and the result.}

* {changed conditions, counterexamples, or what remains unsolved}

{Use condition changes, failure cases or arguments the paper actually tested to state the scope. Keep predictions and measurements apart; where a condition was never validated, write it as unknown rather than completing the authors' results for them. Without a unified mechanism, say how the findings relate and what limits each one.}

{Return to the opening question and use one short paragraph to say how the reader now understands or judges it. Ground it in what came before; add no new evidence, terms or recommendations, and do not force the paper into correcting a misconception it never raised.}
