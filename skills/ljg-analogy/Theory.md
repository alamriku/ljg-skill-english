# Structure mapping: the theoretical basis and the creative trade-offs

Source: Dedre Gentner (1983), *Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy*, Cognitive Science, 7(2), 155-170. [Copy hosted by the author's institution](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/Gentner83.2b.pdf). Page numbers below are the journal's.

## What can be attributed to the paper

| Location | Point | Constraint it places on writing analogies |
|------|------|------------------|
| pp. 157-158 | In "T is like B", B is the base domain whose knowledge is borrowed and T is the target domain to be understood | The direction of search differs from the direction of knowledge transfer; do not reverse what is being explained |
| p. 158 | Object attributes and relations between objects are treated differently; mapping preserves relations first and selects a system of relations | Look for correspondences among roles and their links; a difference in appearance does not by itself defeat an analogy |
| pp. 162-165 | Systematicity concerns interconnected relations organized by higher-order relations | Explain first how the connections hang together; neither the number of relations nor the count of causal words substitutes for that |
| p. 158, p. 162 | What is mapped may be spatial, constraint-based or causal; a metaphor may be relational, attributional or mixed | Do not force every metaphor into a causal account; this skill deliberately focuses on relational metaphors that add understanding |

The theory concerns correspondences in knowledge representation. Representation depends on framing: the same scenario under a different question may present a different set of relations. So "a structure that works was found" does not imply "the one true essence of the matter was found".

## Standards this skill adds

The paper supplies mapping constraints; this skill applies them to finding expressions and explanations: identify the reader's understanding task, compare candidates, preserve the key difference between two objects, require a gain in understanding, and give a specific boundary. These are working standards, not experimental conclusions from Gentner 1983, and they do not constitute an automatic scoring formula.

A mapping that holds shows only that it preserved certain relations; whether those relations are worth preserving depends on the reader's question. Two-object comparisons especially need the check: when both figures hold on their own, do they also preserve the difference between the two objects? Scenes from the same domain often reduce irrelevant variables, but this is not a hard rule; cross-domain distance and novelty are no substitute for explanatory gain.

A footnote on p. 158 of the original says that most explanatory analogies use one-to-one object mapping, while acknowledging exceptions. This skill requires roles to stay fixed within one mapping in order to prevent substitution; that is not grounds for claiming every metaphor must be strictly one-to-one.

## Three situations that are easily misjudged

**All the nouns pair up and the relation is still wrong.** "The editor is a gardener, the article is a tree, cutting words is pruning" only lists pairs. If the user's problem is that different editors keep undoing each other's changes, the load-bearing relation lives in the inconsistent criteria and their back-and-forth effect; talking only about pruning to promote growth hides the original problem.

**The relation is similar and the facts can still differ.** Explaining a kind of loss through a leak transfers the outflow relation under the stated conditions. It does not license the claims that what is lost is conserved like a liquid, that every outlet can be sealed, or that outflow is always bad. Conclusions that need judging go back to the target domain for verification.

**A literary image works, and its explanatory power is still limited.** Using "giraffe" to stress height can be effective rhetoric; it corresponds only on a striking attribute and must not pose as a rich relational mapping. Conversely, a relational metaphor about belonging, waiting or estrangement does not need engineering-style predictions added to count as valid.

## Review the actual deliverable

First ask whether the deliverable helps the reader with this understanding task, then check whether the structure is self-consistent. For a comparative explanation, hand the same new situation to both sides and check whether the analogy supports different but grounded judgments. If swapping the vehicles leaves the explanation intact, that is a sign of weak discrimination; it is not a general automatic scoring rule. Review a literary metaphor on how the relation feels and how it reads, without imposing an operational exercise.

Write out the key roles and relations on both sides and check for a switched identity, a reversed arrow, an inverted sequence, or a "may" turned into a "must". If the new insight depends on a premise the target domain never supplied, that premise should appear explicitly rather than hide inside the rhetoric.

A good boundary changes how the reader uses the figure. "The world is complicated" or "no analogy is exact" cannot tell anyone where it misleads. Pick one limit tied directly to the load-bearing relation.
